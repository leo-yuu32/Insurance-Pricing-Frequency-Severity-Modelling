# Interview notes

Short answers to the questions a pricing actuary is likely to ask about this project, with its actual numbers. The full reasoning is in `docs/DECISIONS.md` (entry numbers in brackets).

## 1. Why a Poisson objective for claim counts, and how is exposure handled in each model?

Claim counts are non-negative integers, and a Poisson model with a log link gives a multiplicative rating structure whose log-likelihood is the natural loss for counts. If claims arrive as a Poisson process, expected claims are exposure times a rate, so log E[N] = log(exposure) + x'beta: log(exposure) is an offset with its coefficient fixed at 1, never a rating factor. In the GLM it is passed to statsmodels as `offset`. In LightGBM it goes in as `init_score = log(exposure x base rate)`; `predict` does not add that back, so the predicted count is base rate x exp(raw score) x exposure, and every validation set needs its own `init_score` (both traps are tested) [D16]. The data are overdispersed (Pearson chi-squared over degrees of freedom 1.69 for the final GLM), which leaves the estimates valid but makes the printed standard errors about 1.3 times too small [D10].

## 2. How does a GBM work, and what do the learning rate, number of leaves and minimum data in leaf control?

A GBM builds small regression trees one after another, each fitted to the gradient of the loss of the current model (for the Poisson loss on the log scale, roughly actual minus predicted claims), and the prediction is the sum of all the trees. The learning rate scales each tree's contribution: a lower rate needs more trees and usually generalises a little better (final GBM: 0.126 with 92 trees). The number of leaves sets how complex one tree can be, and so how many factors one tree can combine (17 here). The minimum data in leaf stops a leaf being fitted to a handful of policies, which matters when only 3.7% of policies have a claim; the final GBM's search chose 1,791, a heavily regularised model [D19, D31].

## 3. How was overfitting prevented?

Every choice (GLM bands, GBM hyperparameters, interaction candidates) was made by 4-fold cross-validation on the training data only, on folds grouped so that policies with identical rating factors stay together. The number of trees was set by early stopping, and the GBM search favoured large leaves and an L2 penalty. The holdout of 133,726 policies was scored once per model, and the final pair's specification was committed before it was scored [D31]. The check that this worked: the final GBM's training deviance is 0.46611, its cross-validated deviance 0.46965 and its holdout deviance 0.46833, so the cross-validation estimate, not the training fit, is what the holdout reproduced.

## 4. What does a SHAP value mean for one policy, and what does a SHAP interaction value add?

For one policy, the GBM's log-frequency prediction equals a base value plus one SHAP value per rating factor; each is that factor's contribution, averaged over the orders in which factors could be added, and in this project they add up to the prediction to within 1e-14. A SHAP value of 0.69 means that factor doubles the policy's predicted frequency. SHAP interaction values split each factor's contribution into its own main effect and a share for each pair of factors, so ranking pairs by their mean absolute interaction gives candidate GLM interactions (the top pair was driver age with bonus-malus, 0.082). These were computed on 2,000 training policies, never the holdout, because they were used to choose model terms [D22, D24].

## 5. Why would a pricing team still use the GLM if the GBM scores better?

A GLM's rating table can be read, challenged and filed: each relativity is one number an underwriter or regulator can check, and it can be implemented exactly in a rating engine (this project's Excel engine reproduces the Python price for 100 of 100 holdout policies). It is also more stable from one rate review to the next and easier to constrain to business rules. In this project the question is partly moot: without `BonusMalus` the GBM's lead is 0.00033 of deviance with an interval that includes zero. Even with `BonusMalus`, most of the GBM's advantage was one piece of structure that could be written as a single GLM term, which is how pricing teams typically use a GBM: as a search tool for the GLM.

## 6. Why is Gini alone not enough to choose a pricing model?

Gini measures only how well a model ranks policies: multiplying every prediction by 10 leaves it unchanged, so it says nothing about whether the price level is right overall or by segment. Both final models have actual over expected of 0.998 overall but about 0.80 on full-year records and 1.43 on short ones, which Gini cannot see. On loss amounts it is dominated by the largest claims: one EUR 4.08 million claim moved the legacy Gini by about 0.18 [D5]. And a higher Gini can come from a factor that contains the outcome: the nine-factor GBM's Gini of 0.320 against the final GBM's 0.183 is mostly `BonusMalus`. Poisson deviance, a proper scoring rule that rewards both ranking and level, is the primary metric here, with actual over expected alongside.

## 7. What does IRLS do at each iteration, why does it equal Newton-Raphson for the Poisson log link but not for the Gamma log link, and how was the from-scratch solver checked against statsmodels?

At each iteration IRLS computes the fitted means mu = exp(X beta + offset), a working response z = X beta + (y - mu) / mu and weights W = w mu^2 / V(mu) (w mu for Poisson, w for Gamma), and solves the weighted least squares problem beta = (X'WX)^-1 X'Wz. That is Fisher scoring: it uses the expected information X'WX. For the Poisson log link, which is canonical, the Hessian -X' diag(mu) X contains no y, so observed and expected information coincide and each step is exactly a Newton-Raphson step. For the Gamma log link (the canonical link is the inverse), the observed Hessian -X' diag(w y / mu) X depends on y, so IRLS takes a different step from Newton-Raphson but reaches the same estimate. The NumPy solver is tested against statsmodels on synthetic data and on the real design matrices: on the final frequency model (534,900 by 55) coefficients agree to 6.0e-12 and standard errors to 9.1e-9 relative, both in 7 iterations, and two further tests confirm the Newton and Fisher statements numerically [D27 to D29].

## 8. Which policies were excluded for having claims without amounts, and why?

The 9,387 policies with `IDpol` up to 24,951: every one has a claim count in the frequency file (9,942 claims in total), but the severity file holds only 307 claim records for them. Either they were selected because they had a claim, so they are not a random sample of the book, or their claim counts are corrupt. Exclusion is right under both readings, whereas counting their claims from the severity records is right only under the second. It costs 1.34% of exposure, and the two choices give portfolio frequencies of 0.0739 and 0.0738, so the decision moves the level by 0.2%; keeping the file's counts would have overstated frequency by 36% [D1].

## 9. What did the additive GBM show about where the GLM-to-GBM gap comes from?

The additive GBM lets each tree split on one factor only, so it has fully flexible one-factor shapes but no interactions. With all nine factors, it closed 84% of the GLM-to-GBM gap (deviance 0.45391 for the GLM, 0.44876 additive, 0.44780 full GBM), leaving 16% for interactions. So the GBM's advantage was mainly the shape of single factors, and the SHAP plots showed which one: the bonus-malus sawtooth, which led to the ladder indicator and then to the timing check [D25, D26]. It was not rebuilt without `BonusMalus`, because the final GBM does not beat the GLM and there is no gap left to split.

## 10. Why is claim frequency not proportional to exposure in this data, and what does that mean for the models?

On the training data the frequency is 0.054 claims per policy-year for full-year records, 0.079 for records of half a year to a year, 0.112 for records under half a year and 0.299 for records of 0.05 years or less. The data do not say why; plausible reasons are that a policy can end, or be split into a new record, after a claim, so short records are partly selected by claims. The log-exposure offset forces proportionality, so both final models over-predict full-year records (actual over expected 0.80) and under-predict short ones (1.43). Exposure is deliberately not a rating factor, because at quotation the insurer knows the planned term, not the observed one, and a model that learned "short record, high risk" would be learning how the data were recorded [D3, D32].

## 11. How was the `BonusMalus` problem found?

The SHAP dependence plot for `BonusMalus` was a sawtooth: scores that can only be reached through a claim (62 is 50 x 1.25) had two to three times the frequency of neighbouring claim-free scores. A one-parameter GLM term for "off the claim-free ladder" then closed 66% of the GLM-to-GBM gap, with a relativity of 2.21. That was a result that looked too good, and the term literally means "has been surcharged for a claim", so the question became whether that claim could be one of the claims being predicted. My first argument that it could not (most claims sit on ladder values) did not hold up, and the direct checks were run before anything was built on the result [D23, D30].

## 12. What did the three checks show, and why are they not proof?

First, the off-ladder effect depends on the length of the record: a relativity of 1.33 for records under half a year, 1.86 for half a year to a year and 3.86 for full years, which a driver characteristic should not do. Second, in full-year records 31.7% of policies at 62 have a claim in the period, against 3.6% at 50, where a prior-claim effect of the size seen in clean-looking data (under 2 times) would give about 7%. Third, which scores carry the excess moves with the policy number: none in the newest eighth of policies, then 62, then 58, then 55 and 52 as well in the oldest, and the newest block still shows no excess on part-year records alone. They are not proof because the dataset does not document when the score was recorded, the reading of `IDpol` as time in the file is an inference, some cells hold fewer than 20 claims, and no single mechanism explains all three patterns; that is why the evidence is described as strong but unproven [D30].

## 13. What does the result without `BonusMalus` mean for GLM against GBM?

Without `BonusMalus` the GLM scores 0.46866 and the GBM 0.46833 on the holdout; the difference of 0.00033 has a 95% bootstrap interval of -0.00019 to 0.00085, so the GBM does not beat the GLM. With the factor, the GBM led by 0.0061 with an interval well clear of zero, so almost all of its advantage came through the fine structure of one factor that may contain the outcome. On the remaining eight factors, a GLM with bands chosen by cross-validation captures what the data support. The statement has to stay narrow: one dataset, one holdout that had been used before, so "a tuned GBM did not beat the GLM here", not "GBMs cannot beat GLMs". It also comes at a cost: dropping the factor cuts the deviance explained from 4.85% to 1.75%, and a real insurer, which knows each driver's coefficient at the start of the policy, would use it [D32].
